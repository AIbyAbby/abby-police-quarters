故事散步地圖──《房子記得，我也記得》：17個點的記憶

我又走回這條街。風吹過來的時候，有一種味道，很淡，但好像從記憶裡滲出來。

走到「生活綠廊小公園」的時候，我停了下來。以前這裡是警察宿舍，院子裡擺滿了盆栽，每一盆都像是有人偷偷放進去的小心願。現在盆栽不見了，只剩下搖晃的樹影，可我總覺得，它們還在等誰回家。

轉過那個角落，就是「金石堂和搖滾披薩」。這裡以前是診所和藥廠，藥味會從門縫裡飄出來；而現在，換成了烤箱裡的麵香，熱騰騰的，把整條街都暖了起來。味道變了，可那種守護的心意，好像沒有變過。

再抬頭，我看到「海天公寓」。高樓的確很氣派，可它像一堵牆，把過去那些鄰居的笑聲都擋住了。那時候的笑聲，現在好像只剩風在陽台上穿來穿去。

我轉進「博愛街9巷」。那棟廢棄的宿舍還站在那裡，屋瓦塌了，窗花也裂了。可我不知道為什麼，總覺得空氣裡有人輕輕地說：「我回來了。」

「博愛街11巷和13巷的小公園」倒是熱鬧得多。孩子們的腳步聲，就像一把鑰匙，把藏在風裡的笑聲都打開了。我往裡走，看見「博愛街13巷的龍眼樹」還穩穩地站著。它像一個沉默的老人，看了好多個夏天來來去去，卻一直守著那座已經不在的房子。

再走到「博愛新村的甲棟和乙棟」。我忍不住抬頭看那些整齊的窗。三代人在這裡吃飯、做夢、吵架，那些日子平凡得不得了，可比任何高樓都要厚重。

繼續走，我看到「博愛街13巷和25巷之間的廢棄宿舍」。花草早就枯了，可我好像還聽得到那些夜深回家的腳步聲，特別是那種怕吵醒家人而輕輕關門的聲音。

「博愛街13巷口的大胖ㄟ水果攤」完全不一樣。老闆的笑聲像是一盞燈，照亮了整條街，也照亮了我的記憶。

「博愛街25巷」的宿舍只剩下一面面斷牆，可那棵龍眼樹還在。它孤單卻很倔強，好像不肯放下那些過去的人。

來到「瑠公圳綠地公園」。我先看到「芒果樹」，枝頭還掛著午後的陽光。再往裡，是「原警察宿舍」的位置，現在長滿青草，好像整片土地深深吸了一口氣。最後走到「櫻花樹」前。春天的時候，它開得燦爛極了，就像老房子推開窗，對我說：「你好久不見。」

「北新路二段29巷」的草地變得很安靜。時間好像在那裡放下了什麼，可又留住了什麼。而「臨時小公園四」的芒果樹早就被砍掉了。天空突然變得好空，可我明白，那棵樹從來不只是棵樹，它曾是我們的守護神。

我回到「博愛街6巷和8巷的小公園」。這裡以前是最吵鬧的宿舍，現在連風都吹得特別小心。最後，我停在「博愛街19巷」。那裡很快就要都更了。有人感嘆：「它們要拆了。」可燈還亮著，好像有人在告訴房子：「記得我最後的樣子。」

我走完整條街，心裡突然覺得很溫暖。那些房子、樹木、還有笑聲，雖然不再如初，但它們都還在，用自己的方式守著這裡。這些記憶，好像在輕輕地對我說：

「謝謝你回來，我們一直都在。」

A Walk Down Memory Lane

The Houses Remember, and So Do I

I find myself walking down this street again. As the wind blows, it carries a faint scent, one that seems to seep out of my memories.

I stop at the

"Living Green Corridor Mini-Park."

This used to be the police dormitory, and the yard was filled with potted plants, each one like a quiet wish someone had secretly placed there. Now the pots are gone, leaving only the swaying shadows of the trees. Yet, I can't shake the feeling that they are still waiting for someone to come home.

Turning the corner, I see

"Kingstone Bookstore and Rockin' Pizza."

This place was once a clinic and a pharmaceutical factory, where the smell of medicine would drift through the doors. Now, it's the warm, doughy scent from the oven, filling the whole street with warmth. The smells have changed, but that sense of guardianship, that feeling of being watched over, seems to remain.

I look up and see

"Haitian Apartments."

The high-rise is certainly impressive, but it feels like a wall, blocking out the laughter of the old neighbors. That laughter, now, feels like only the wind passing through the balconies.

I turn into

"Bo-Ai Street, Lane 9."

The abandoned dormitory is still standing, its tiles collapsed and window frames cracked. But for some reason, I feel as if someone is softly whispering in the air, "I'm back."

The

"Mini-Parks in Bo-Ai Street, Lanes 11 and 13"

are much livelier. The sound of children's footsteps acts like a key, unlocking the laughter hidden in the wind. As I walk in, I see the

"longan tree in Bo-Ai Street, Lane 13"

standing firm. It's like a silent old man who has watched countless summers come and go, still guarding the house that is no longer there.

I continue to the

"A and B Buildings of Bo-Ai New Village."

I can't help but look up at the neat rows of windows. Three generations ate, dreamed, and argued here. Those days were so ordinary, yet they feel more substantial than any high-rise.

Further on, I see the

"abandoned dormitories between Bo-Ai Street, Lanes 13 and 25."

The flowers and plants have long withered, but I feel as though I can still hear the footsteps of people returning home late at night—especially that soft closing of a door, careful not to wake the family.

The

"Big Fatty Fruit Stand at the entrance of Bo-Ai Street, Lane 13"

is completely different. The owner's laughter is like a lamp, illuminating the entire street and my memories.

The dormitories in

"Bo-Ai Street, Lane 25"

are reduced to fragmented walls, but that longan tree remains. Lonely yet stubborn, it seems unwilling to let go of the people of the past.

I arrive at the

"Liugong Canal Greenbelt Park."

First, I see the

"mango tree,"

its branches still holding onto the afternoon sun. Deeper inside, where the

"original police dormitory"

stood, tall grass now grows, as if the whole piece of land has taken a deep breath. Finally, I walk to the

"cherry blossom tree."

In the spring, it bloomed so brightly, like an old house pushing open a window to say to me, "It's been a long time."

The lawn on

"Beixin Road, Section 2, Lane 29"

has become very quiet. Time seems to have left something there, yet held on to something else. And the mango tree in

"Temporary Mini-Park Four"

has long been cut down. The sky suddenly feels so empty, but I understand that the tree was never just a tree; it was our guardian.

I return to the

"Mini-Parks in Bo-Ai Street, Lanes 6 and 8."

This used to be the noisiest dormitory, and now even the wind blows with special care. Finally, I stop at

"Bo-Ai Street, Lane 19."

It will soon be redeveloped. Someone sighs, "They're going to tear them down." But the light is still on, as if someone is telling the house, "Remember me as I am now, for the last time."

As I finish my walk down the street, my heart suddenly feels very warm. The houses, the trees, and the laughter—though no longer as they were—are all still here, guarding this place in their own way. These memories seem to be softly whispering to me:

"Thank you for coming back. We've been here all along."
